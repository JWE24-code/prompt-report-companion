Overview

What the paper is and how it was made. Paper §1, §2.1

Prompting has grown fast, and papers often use the same word for different things. The authors set out to fix the vocabulary and catalog the techniques. They searched arXiv, Semantic Scholar and ACL with 44 keywords, followed the PRISMA process for systematic reviews, and used both human annotators and a GPT-4 classifier to filter the results. The final dataset holds 1,565 papers and is published on Hugging Face.

The study is deliberately narrow. It covers prefix prompts (the model continues from your text), hard prompts made of real words rather than learned vectors, and task-agnostic techniques. Fine-tuning and gradient-based methods are mostly left out.

Human annotators agreed 92% of the time on 300 shared papers (Krippendorff's alpha 81%). The GPT-4 filter reached 89% precision and 75% recall against 100 ground-truth labels.

                  Case study: prompting for suicidal-crisis signals

                  A worked example of prompt engineering on a hard, sensitive task. Paper §6.2

                  The authors asked how well a prompt can flag entrapment (frantic hopelessness) in posts from r/SuicideWatch, a key signal in Suicide Crisis Syndrome. Two trained coders labeled 221 posts (Krippendorff's alpha 0.72). The prompt engineer, who is the paper's lead author, used 121 posts for development and held out 100 for testing.

                  The process took 47 recorded steps, about 20 hours. It started at 0% because outputs were not parseable, and ended at an F1 of 0.53 (precision 0.86, recall 0.38). The authors say they present it as an illustration of the craft, not as a solved problem, and that F1's equal weight on precision and recall may not suit this domain.

                  Two lessons stand out. Safety guardrails on some models caused the LLM to offer mental health advice instead of labeling the text, which pushed the engineer to switch models. And results moved by as much as 0.04 F1 between repeated runs even at temperature zero.

                  This topic is sensitive. The study is research on text classification and is not a clinical tool. If you or someone you know is struggling, please contact local emergency services or a crisis line in your country.
